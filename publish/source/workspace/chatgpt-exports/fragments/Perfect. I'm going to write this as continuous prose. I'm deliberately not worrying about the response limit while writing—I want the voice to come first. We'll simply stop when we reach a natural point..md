# Chapter Two
# Fallcrest
The road followed the River Nentir for the last few miles before reaching Fallcrest. After the loneliness of the northern forest, the countryside seemed almost impossibly peaceful. Fields of barley rippled in the evening breeze, orchards heavy with fruit lined the lower slopes, and cattle grazed lazily in rich green meadows beside the river. Farmers worked until the last of the daylight, while children ran laughing between haystacks, their parents only half-heartedly calling them back to help.
For the first time since leaving home, the companions were walking through a land that felt settled.
There were no ruined watchtowers hidden amongst the trees. No warnings scratched into old milestones. No feeling that danger might be waiting around the next bend in the road.
Even Mara seemed to notice the change. During the journey from the goblin cave she had kept one hand on the satchel hanging at her shoulder almost constantly, as though checking every few minutes that it was still there. Now, without realising it, she had finally let her arm fall naturally by her side.
"I'd forgotten how much I'd missed this," she said quietly.
Dave looked around at the fields.
"The sheep?"
Mara laughed.
"No."
"The feeling."
She took a slow breath.
"When you travel all the time you stop noticing places that feel safe."
Thorn understood exactly what she meant, although he couldn't have explained why. There was something about the valley that made it feel lived in. The roads were well maintained. Dry stone walls had stood for generations. Even the hedgerows had been carefully trimmed. It wasn't exciting.
It was home.
The road climbed gently over one final ridge before beginning its descent towards the town. Mara stopped without saying a word, and the others naturally gathered beside her.
Below them lay Fallcrest.
The town occupied one of the finest defensive positions Thorn had ever seen. The River Nentir curved around its southern edge before plunging over spectacular waterfalls farther downstream, while a broad bluff of weathered grey stone rose nearly two hundred feet above the river, dividing the settlement into two distinct halves. Roads from every direction converged here before crossing the river and continuing south into the heart of Almanor, making Fallcrest a natural meeting place for merchants, travellers and soldiers alike.
The lower town spread along the riverbank in a maze of narrow streets, workshops, warehouses and inns. Barges drifted steadily along the water as cranes unloaded timber, grain and barrels onto the busy quays. Even from this distance the companions could hear the sounds of trade drifting across the valley—the clatter of wagon wheels, the ringing of blacksmiths' hammers and the distant calls of dockworkers finishing the day's labour.
Above the bluff stood the older part of the town.
Stone houses lined broad streets that followed the edge of the cliff, while a curtain wall enclosed the northern side where Moonstone Keep overlooked the valley below. Near the eastern edge of the bluff, rising well above every other building, stood a tall tower built from pale green stone that caught the evening light differently from everything around it.
Dave saw it immediately.
"That's Septarch's Tower."
It wasn't really a question.
Mara followed his gaze and smiled.
"It is."
"And Nimozaran lives there?"
"He does."
Dave stared at the tower for several moments before asking the question that had clearly been waiting in his mind since they'd left the goblin cave.
"Have you ever met him?"
"I've delivered messages there plenty of times," Mara replied as they resumed walking. "Although 'met' might be stretching things a little."
"What do you mean?"
"I've met Tobbs dozens of times."
"The apprentice?"
She nodded.
"He's usually the one who answers the door. As for Nimozaran himself..." She considered the question for a moment before smiling to herself. "I'm still not entirely certain whether he notices people arriving or simply notices that books have stopped arriving."
Derek chuckled.
"So he's absent-minded?"
"Oh, I don't think so. I think he's thinking about things that nobody else can even imagine."
Dave's eyes drifted back towards the tower.
"I'd still like to meet him."
"I rather thought you might."
As they descended the hill, the road became steadily busier. Farmers returning from the fields exchanged greetings with merchants heading for the town before the gates closed. A wagon carrying quarried stone creaked slowly uphill behind four enormous oxen, while a pair of dwarven traders argued cheerfully over whose mule had done the greater share of the work despite both animals carrying almost identical loads.
Loki approved of all of them.
The big dog wandered happily from one interesting smell to another until Thorn finally whistled him back from a passing butcher's cart. The butcher merely laughed, snapped off a small piece of sausage and tossed it towards him.
Loki caught it neatly in mid-air.
"You encourage him," Thorn called apologetically.
The butcher shrugged.
"He looked disappointed."
"He usually does."
"It's difficult to disappoint a dog with sausage."
Loki trotted back looking enormously pleased with both himself and the world in general.
"He has a gift," Mara observed.
"What sort of gift?" Dave asked.
"Making everyone believe they're already friends."
Thorn scratched behind the dog's ears.
"I've never known him to think otherwise."
The western gate stood open, watched by two guards wearing the green and silver colours of Fallcrest. One of them recognised Mara before she had even reached the archway.
"Mara!"
Relief spread instantly across the man's face.
"By the gods, we heard about the ambush. Sergeant Murgeddin sent riders looking for you yesterday."
"I know."
She smiled warmly.
"I'm sorry to worry everyone."
The guard looked from Mara to the companions standing behind her.
"So these are the folk responsible."
"They're the reason I'm standing here."
He stepped forward and offered Thorn his hand.
"Then Fallcrest owes every one of you its thanks."
Thorn accepted the handshake, feeling slightly awkward beneath the attention.
"We just happened to be on the road."
"So I've heard."
The guard smiled knowingly.
"My father always said that whenever someone claims they 'just happened' to save a life, they're usually being far too modest."
Nibbles leaned quietly towards Dave.
"I like this town."
"You've only just arrived."
"I know."
"So far everyone's either fed Loki or thanked us."
He considered that for a moment.
"Excellent first impressions."
Passing beneath the old stone gatehouse felt like crossing into another world.
The streets beyond bustled with life. Merchants were packing away market stalls as the day drew to a close, calling out final bargains to anyone still lingering nearby. Apprentices hurried home carrying armfuls of tools, while neighbours paused in doorways to exchange gossip before the evening meal. Somewhere close by someone was baking bread, and the warm smell drifted through the streets, mixing with woodsmoke, horses and the clean scent of the river.
Dave slowed almost to a stop.
"I've never seen so many people."
Mara looked around fondly.
"This is a quiet evening."
He stared at her.
"...You're joking."
"I'm really not."
They wandered through Lowtown at an easy pace, allowing Mara to point out the places every resident took for granted. She showed them the market square, the riverside warehouses and the busy quays where goods from across the Nentir Vale were unloaded before being carried through the town. Every few streets she seemed to meet someone she knew—a baker who asked after her father, an elderly ferryman who insisted she still owed him a pie from three summers ago, and a cooper who looked genuinely delighted to discover she had survived the journey north.
The more Thorn looked around, however, the more he noticed something else.
Fallcrest carried its history in plain sight.
Many of the buildings were new, but they stood amongst much older ruins. Broken arches had become garden walls. Roofless halls now sheltered workshops built within their crumbling stone shells. In one small square an enormous cracked pillar rose alone into the sky, surrounded by houses that had simply been built around it rather than replacing it.
Mara saw him studying the old stonework.
"The Bloodspear orcs reached Fallcrest a hundred years ago," she said quietly. "Most of Lowtown burned before the defenders finally drove them back."
She rested her fingers against one weathered wall as they walked past.
"My grandfather was born after the war, but his father helped rebuild these streets. He used to say that every stone in Fallcrest had been lifted twice."
Thorn looked around once more.
"It doesn't feel like a ruined town."
"No."
She smiled.
"It feels like a town that refused to stay ruined."
The climb to Hightown began at a broad flight of stone steps carved directly into the face of the bluff. Halfway up, Dave slowed dramatically.
"I'm just..."
He paused to catch his breath.
"...admiring the view."
Derek looked down at him.
"You needed a rest."
"I did."
"But the view is also excellent."
Thorn smiled to himself as they continued climbing, the evening sun stretching long shadows across the rooftops below while the great green tower of Septarch's Tower watched silently over the town.

By the time they reached the top of the bluff, the sun had dipped low enough that the windows of Hightown glowed like little squares of fire. The noise of Lowtown still rose from below—the clatter of carts, the distant call of boatmen, the constant movement of the quays—but up here it softened into background music. Hightown had a different rhythm. Its streets were wider, its buildings older and sturdier, and the people moved with the calm purpose of those who lived behind walls they trusted.
Mara led them first to the Nentir Inn, a handsome building of fieldstone and dark timber standing near the western side of Hightown. It was not grand in the way a noble’s hall might be grand, but it had the more important quality of looking immediately comfortable. Warm light shone from its windows, and the smell drifting from inside suggested stew, fresh bread and the sort of kitchen where nobody left hungry.
The innkeeper came around the counter the moment she saw Mara. She was a stern-looking woman at first glance, with silver threaded through her dark hair and the expression of someone who had dealt with every kind of traveller imaginable, but her face softened so quickly that Thorn knew at once she and Mara were friends.
“Mara,” she said, pulling her into a fierce embrace. “I was beginning to think the road had taken you.”
“It nearly did,” Mara replied, hugging her back. When she stepped away, she turned slightly so the woman could see the companions. “These are the reason it didn’t.”
The innkeeper studied them properly then: Thorn with his axe, Derek with his shield, Dave still dusty from the road and clutching his spellbook a little too protectively, Nibbles already examining the room as though every cupboard might conceal treasure, and Loki sitting at Thorn’s side with the hopeful dignity of a dog who believed all buildings contained food.
“Well,” the woman said, crouching to scratch Loki behind the ears, “this one is clearly trustworthy.”
Loki accepted the compliment with solemn grace.
“My name is Wisara,” she continued, standing again. “You’ll stay here tonight. Rooms upstairs. Supper if you need it, though Mara has probably already planned to drag you all down to the Blue Moon. Breakfast in the morning. No charge.”
Nibbles, who had been perfectly happy until the last two words, narrowed his eyes. “No charge?”
Wisara looked at him patiently. “No charge.”
“That usually means there’s a catch.”
“It means you saved my friend.”
Nibbles considered this. “That’s a surprisingly good reason.”
“It’s the only one you’re getting.”
The rooms upstairs were plain but clean, and after days of travelling even plain felt luxurious. The beds had thick blankets. The washbasin contained water that was not from a puddle. There were shutters that closed properly and floors that did not slope alarmingly towards a cave wall. Dave sat on the edge of one mattress and pressed both hands into it as though testing whether it was real.
“I think,” he said, “that civilisation may have been invented mainly for beds.”
Derek set his pack down beside the door. “And roofs.”
“And breakfast,” Nibbles added from the next room.
Thorn smiled as he rinsed the dust from his hands. For a short while, it was enough simply to be safe. No goblin arrows. No damp caves. No shouting in the dark. Just warm water, clean linen, and Loki lying happily across the doorway so that anyone entering would have to step over him first.
Mara left them with instructions to meet her at the Blue Moon before sunset, which gave them a little time to see more of Fallcrest. They did not so much decide to explore together as drift out into the streets in the same direction, unwilling yet to separate completely in a town none of them knew. Hightown was settling into evening around them. Lamps were being lit. Shopkeepers were bringing wares inside. Somewhere nearby, a child was being called home for the third time and pretending not to hear.
They found Teldorthan’s Arms by following the sound of hammering. The weaponsmith’s yard stood near the keep, where the smell of coal smoke and hot iron hung in the air. Several apprentices worked at the forge while an elderly dwarf sat on a stool with a pipe clenched between his teeth, watching them with the relaxed confidence of someone who knew exactly what everyone was doing wrong.
He noticed Thorn looking at the weapons on display and waved him closer with the stem of his pipe. “Don’t hover in the doorway, lad. Makes you look like you’re planning to steal something but haven’t decided what.”
“I’m not stealing anything,” Thorn said.
“Good. Saves us both trouble.” The dwarf’s eyes dropped to the axe at Thorn’s back. “You already own a weapon.”
“I want to commission one.”
“Course you do. Young fighters always want something sharper, heavier, shinier or more likely to impress people who don’t know better.” Teldorthan held out one hand. “Let’s see it.”
Thorn hesitated only a moment before passing over his axe. The old dwarf’s manner changed as soon as he took it. He tested the balance, examined the head, ran one thumb carefully along the edge and studied the haft where years of use had darkened the wood.
“Family weapon?” he asked.
“My father’s.”
Teldorthan nodded, and when he spoke again his voice had lost some of its teasing edge. “Then you’re not replacing it. You’re continuing it.”
Thorn looked at him, surprised.
The dwarf handed the axe back. “A weapon like that is a story. Doesn’t mean the story can’t have a new chapter, but you don’t throw away the first pages.” He called one of his apprentices over and began speaking about steel, balance, haft length and the sort of blade shape that could take a shield apart without becoming too heavy to use all day. Thorn listened more carefully than he had listened to almost anything since leaving home.
Dave, meanwhile, kept glancing east whenever Septarch’s Tower appeared between the rooftops. He was not subtle about it. After the fourth time, Derek sighed and said, “You can look at the tower properly if you want.”
“I wasn’t—”
“You were.”
“I just wondered whether magical towers have shops nearby.”
Nibbles brightened. “That is the best excuse you’ve ever made.”
Mara had mentioned that anyone seeking magical supplies in Fallcrest usually tried Naerumar’s Imports, so they made their way through the streets until they found the shop. It was finer than most of the others, its windows displaying gemstones, painted boxes, silver chains, carved bone charms and several objects whose purpose was not immediately clear. A polished sign above the door bore the name in elegant letters.
Inside, the shop smelled faintly of incense, dust and expensive things.
Orest Naerumar stood behind the counter, a lean tiefling with dark red skin, swept-back horns and the expression of someone who had never once been impressed by a customer. His clothes were immaculate, his manners precise, and his patience apparently limited.
“Yes?” he said, looking at them as though they had tracked mud onto his floor before checking whether they actually had.
Dave stepped forward with unusual seriousness. “I’m looking for magical ink. For copying spells.”
Naerumar’s eyes flicked to the spellbook under Dave’s arm. “Apprentice?”
“Wizard,” Dave said, then added, “learning.”
“A wise correction.” The tiefling reached beneath the counter and produced a small crystal bottle filled with deep blue ink that shimmered when it caught the light. “This will serve, if you can afford it.”
Nibbles leaned closer. “That’s a very small bottle.”
Naerumar looked at him. “And yet it remains more valuable than most large things.”
“That seems unfair.”
“So does poverty,” Naerumar replied.
Nibbles opened his mouth, clearly preparing something unwise, but before he could speak two very large tiefling bodyguards stepped into view from a doorway behind the counter. Neither said a word. They did not need to. One had arms like carved oak and the other wore an expression suggesting he had been waiting all day for someone to provide a reason.
Nibbles closed his mouth.
Dave became suddenly fascinated by counting out coins correctly.
Thorn stood a little straighter.
Derek gave Naerumar a polite nod. “A fine establishment.”
Naerumar’s thin smile suggested he appreciated the improvement in tone. “I have always thought so.”
The transaction became much smoother after that. Dave paid for the ink with the care of someone purchasing a dragon egg, while Nibbles asked no further questions at all until they were safely back on the street.
“I didn’t like him,” the rogue said.
“You became very polite very quickly,” Derek observed.
“I respect local customs.”
“The local custom being enormous bodyguards?”
“Exactly.”
They had not gone far when Dave slowed beside the town stables. Several horses stood in a paddock near the road, switching their tails and cropping at hay while a stablehand brushed down a sturdy bay mare. For a moment Dave looked from the horses to the road beyond the town walls with the expression of someone having a practical thought.
“We could buy horses,” he said.
Nibbles immediately approved of the idea. “I would look excellent on a horse.”
“You’d fall off,” Derek said.
“I would fall off dramatically.”
The stablemaster, a weathered halfling with straw on one sleeve, came over to ask whether they were looking to buy or hire. He had barely finished the sentence when one of the horses noticed Loki. The horse lifted its head, stared at the dog, and gave a nervous snort. Loki, delighted by the possibility of another new friend, wagged his tail.
The horse backed away.
Another horse noticed what the first horse was doing and decided to be nervous as well. Within moments the entire paddock had become a shifting mess of stamping hooves, flattened ears and suspicious glances in Loki’s direction. Loki sat down, confused and slightly hurt.
“He’s very friendly,” Thorn said apologetically.
“I’m sure he is,” the stablemaster replied, watching one horse attempt to hide behind another. “Unfortunately, they are not.”
“So no horses?” Dave asked.
“Not unless your dog plans to travel separately.”
Loki gave a small bark, as though objecting to the entire idea.
Nibbles sighed as they left the stables behind. “There goes my dramatic entrance.”
“You can still walk dramatically,” Derek told him.
“I already do.”
By then the light was beginning to fade. The lamps of Hightown glowed warmly behind windows, and the noise from Lowtown drifted up the bluff with the smell of cooking fires and river water. Somewhere below, the Blue Moon Alehouse would already be filling with traders, boatmen, farmers and townsfolk looking for supper, music and news. Mara was waiting for them there, and although none of them knew it yet, so was the first true sign that the map she carried had placed them in far greater danger than any goblin cave.
